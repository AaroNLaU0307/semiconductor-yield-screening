# Replication and drift: why the associations did not transfer

**This analysis is post-hoc.** It was not declared at Gate 1. It was added after the predictive model failed to transfer to the holdout, in order to explain why. It changes no frozen quantity, and it reuses the holdout that DECISIONS.md D12 already released for model evaluation rather than opening anything new. Read everything here as a hypothesis generated by the data, not as a confirmed result.

## 1. The question

The screen found 32 features associated with failure in the train period, and the permutation null said that count was not chance (p = 0.0005; p = 0.0319 under the post-hoc null that keeps the labels in time order, `reports/screening.md` section 4). The model built on those features then failed to beat no skill on the holdout. Both statements are correct, and this page reconciles them.

## 2. The associations mostly do not reappear

| property | value |
| --- | --- |
| shortlisted features | 32 |
| keeping the same effect sign on holdout | 15 of 32 |
| expected by chance if the sign were random | 16 |
| correlation between train and holdout effect size | 0.216 |
| median absolute effect, train | 0.2102 |
| median absolute effect, holdout | 0.0774 |
| shrinkage in median absolute effect | 63.2% |

The sign agreement of 15 out of 32 is what a coin would produce. The median absolute effect size falls by 63% across the boundary. Whatever the screen measured in the train period, most of it is not present in the holdout period.


## 3. But not all of it vanishes

**2 of 32 shortlisted features do replicate on the holdout period under BH control at q = 0.1, applied to the family of 32 shortlisted features rather than to the original 424:** `sensor_103` (train +0.294, holdout +0.430, adjusted p 0.0073), `sensor_510` (train +0.240, holdout +0.339, adjusted p 0.0587). These are the candidates a process engineer should look at first, because they are the only ones that survived a change of time period.


**What to compare that against.** The family here is the 32 shortlisted features, not the original 424. Under the complete null, BH at q = 0.1 rejects at least once with probability at most 0.1, so a chance yield of two survivors is unlikely though not impossible. Testing the same 32 uncorrected at alpha = 0.05 would produce about 1.6 by chance alone, which is why the correction is applied rather than the raw p-values quoted.


Both intervals in the table below exclude zero. That is a stronger statement than the p-values alone, and it is reported because an effect size estimated from 26 holdout failures is not a result without one. The intervals are wide, and they are wide for a reason that cannot be fixed by analysis.


Two cautions remain. The count is small, and a post-hoc replication check on a period already used for model evaluation is weaker evidence than a pre-declared test on fresh data. Confirming these would need a third time period that no part of this project has touched.


### Are these two contaminated by the sampling bias?

`reports/data_quality.md` finds that two metrology sampling plans are themselves associated with failure, and warns that any sensor shortlisted from those same lots inherits the concern. These two features are the only positive output of this project, so that warning is discharged for them explicitly rather than left hanging.


**They are not drawn from a sampled subpopulation.** Both are dense band sensors measured on essentially every lot:

| feature | train lots measured | holdout lots measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sensor_103 | 0.99909 | 0.99788 |
| sensor_510 | 0.99909 | 0.99788 |

**And the association survives holding each sampling plan fixed.** Recomputing the effect within each stratum of every sampling plan that cleared FDR: an association that were really the sampling plan in disguise would disappear once the plan is held constant. It does not. Every stratum keeps the same sign.

| sensor_103 | sensor_072 | measured | 164 | 9 | 0.2222 | 0.2645 |

The sign and magnitude are consistent throughout, so sampling bias is excluded as the explanation for these two rather than merely argued to be inapplicable.


## 4. The candidate explanation: the sensors drift with time

| property | value |
| --- | --- |
| features with strong time trend on train, absolute Spearman above 0.3 | 16 of 32 |
| median absolute correlation with time | 0.2799 |

The failure rate itself moves by a factor of seven across the collection window (DECISIONS.md D0). Many of the shortlisted sensors move steadily with time as well. When two quantities both trend over a window, a univariate test computed inside that window sees a strong association whether or not either quantity has anything to do with the other. That is enough to produce a real, reproducible, permutation-confirmed screen result that nonetheless carries no predictive content in a later period.


The clearest single case is the top ranked sensor. Its train effect is the largest in the screen, its holdout effect is near zero, and its own monthly mean falls steadily across the four months of the record, in the same direction as the failure rate. Nothing about that pattern requires the sensor to influence yield.


Monthly mean of the most strongly time trending shortlisted sensors, plus the largest effect sensor discussed above, from `sql/monthly_sensor_means.sql` (6 sensors). Read it against the monthly failure rate in DECISIONS.md D0, which runs 0.2222, 0.0919, 0.0288, 0.0613 over the same months:

## 6. What this means for the project

The screen is not wrong, and the permutation null is not invalidated. Both answer the question they were asked, which is whether the train period contains more association than chance would produce. They were not asked whether that association is stable over time, and it mostly is not.


The practical consequence is that a screen of this kind should be paired with a stability check across time blocks before its output is handed to an engineer. DECISIONS.md A4 records this as the main thing that would be done differently.
